# Why this matters now

Cyber risk used to be reported as a list of tools and tickets. Boards are now expected to **oversee** it the way they oversee
financial risk: with defined ownership, regular reporting and evidence that the reporting is true.

> Oversight is not the same as reassurance. A clean dashboard is a prompt for questions, not an answer.

## What changed

- Disclosure clocks are short, so the decision-making path must exist *before* an incident.
- Insurers ask for evidence of controls, not intentions.
- Customers send security questionnaires during procurement and treat the answers as contract terms.

# Five questions for management

| # | Question | What a good answer sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Who owns cyber risk, and who do they report to? | A named executive with a direct line to the CEO or board committee |
| 2 | What are our three largest cyber exposures in dollars? | A ranked list with ranges and the assumptions behind them |
| 3 | When did we last test incident response end to end? | A dated exercise with findings and owners for each |
| 4 | Which vendors can stop our business if they fail? | A tiered list with contract and recovery terms reviewed this year |
| 5 | What would we tell regulators within four days? | A drafted decision path and the people who run it |
: Questions that move a board from reassurance to oversight

::: {.warning title="Watch for"}
Answers that describe **activity** (patches applied, alerts closed) instead of **exposure** (what could we lose, and how fast could we recover).
:::

# Recommendation

::: {.decision title="Recommendation"}
Add a standing cyber-risk item to the audit and risk committee agenda, with a one-page report in business terms.
:::
